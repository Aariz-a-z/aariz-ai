# Fuel Oil Treatment and Bunkering

The vessel carries heavy fuel oil for sea passage and marine gas oil for port operation, emission control area transit and the auxiliary boiler. This instruction covers storage, treatment and bunkering. Combustion behaviour in the main engine is covered separately.

## Heating and viscosity

Heavy fuel oil is heated progressively through the system. Bunker tanks are maintained at a temperature sufficient to keep the oil pumpable. The settling tank is held at a higher temperature to promote separation of water and sediment by gravity. Final heating before the injection pumps raises the oil to ninety-eight degrees Celsius, which corresponds to a viscosity of approximately twelve centistokes at the engine inlet.

Viscosity, not temperature, is the controlled variable. The viscometer regulates the final heater, and the temperature figure above is the value normally observed for the grade in service rather than a set point in its own right. A different grade will reach twelve centistokes at a different temperature.

## The fuel oil purifier

The fuel oil purifier operates at ninety-eight degrees Celsius. Separation efficiency falls sharply below ninety degrees because the density difference between oil and water narrows as the oil cools. Throughput must be set so that the purifier processes slightly more than the engine consumes, allowing the service tank to remain full without excessive recirculation.

The purifier is fitted with a gravity disc selected for the density of the fuel in service. Fitting a disc intended for a lighter grade will cause the oil and water interface to move outward and oil will be lost to the water side. This is the most common cause of unexplained fuel consumption discrepancies.

Note that the lubricating oil purifier operates at a different temperature and has its own instruction. The two machines are physically similar and the settings are not interchangeable.

## Settling and service tanks

The settling tank is drained of water and sludge at the start of every watch and the quantity recorded. Draining is not to be deferred to a convenient time; water carried forward to the service tank will reach the injection equipment. The service tank is drained daily.

Sludge from draining and from purifier discharge is pumped to the sludge tank. Disposal of sludge and the associated record keeping are covered in the bilge and oily water instruction, which also covers the discharge limits that apply to oily mixtures.

## Sulphur limits and changeover

The sulphur content of fuel oil used outside an emission control area must not exceed 0.50 per cent by mass. Inside an emission control area the limit is 0.10 per cent by mass. Changeover to compliant fuel must be completed before the vessel enters the area, and the time, date and position at which changeover was completed are recorded in the engine log.

Changeover is carried out slowly. The temperature of the fuel entering the injection pumps must not fall by more than two degrees Celsius per minute, because a rapid change causes differential contraction between the pump plunger and barrel and can seize the pump. This rate limit is the reason changeover is begun well before the area boundary rather than at it.

## Bunkering

Before bunkering, the plan is agreed with the supplier, scuppers are plugged, and the overflow tank is verified empty. A responsible officer remains at the manifold throughout. Tank soundings are taken before and after by the vessel's own staff.

A representative sample is drawn continuously throughout the delivery. The sample is sealed in the presence of the supplier's representative and retained on board for twelve months. The bunker delivery note is retained for three years. These two periods are different and are frequently confused; the sample is twelve months and the delivery note is three years.

If the delivered fuel is found off specification after the supplier has departed, the retained sample is the only evidence available. Nothing about the sample handling may be delegated to the supplier.
